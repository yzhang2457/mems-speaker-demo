What you might hear

Simplified sketch of our ear-simulator measurements, levels matched at 1 kHz.

  • IE 100 Pro: stronger bass. Like most consumer earphones, it is tuned with a deliberate bass boost, so kick drums and bass lines sound fuller.
  • MEMS microspeaker: more treble. Its bass is flatter, and a small resonance peak near 10 kHz makes cymbals, hi-hats, breaths and “s” sounds stand out more.
  • Neither is “correct”. Listeners differ, and so does music: you may prefer one device for drums and the other for vocals or acoustic tracks.

How these were recorded

  • Both speakers played the same tracks at the same drive level into an IEC 60318-4 ear simulator (1.26 cc) inside an anechoic chamber.
  • Both recordings were processed identically and loudness-normalized to −14 LUFS to preserve dynamic range. (Modern music often exceeds −10 LUFS.)
  • An ear simulator is not a human ear, so this is a side-by-side comparison, not exactly what you would hear wearing either earphone.

About the microspeaker

A 26 mm² corrugated Parylene-D diaphragm with an electroplated Cu coil over an NdFeB magnet, batch-fabricated on silicon. The diaphragm chip plus magnet is only ~0.6 mm thick, and the sensitivity is about 110 dB SPL/Vrms at 1 kHz with no DC bias.

See the diaphragm move

Driven at 4 Hz with 4 Vpp, a slow, large signal that makes the motion visible: the whole diaphragm and coil move up and down together as a piston. At audio frequencies the motion is far smaller and too fast to see. (Video at 10 frames/s, perspective-corrected and cropped.)
